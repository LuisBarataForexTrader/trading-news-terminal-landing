PCE vs CPI

Both track how fast US prices rise, but they come from different agencies and move markets in different ways. Here is how CPI and Core PCE differ, and how active traders read them together.

Start here

Both gauges answer the same question in different ways

If you follow markets, you hear two inflation numbers quoted constantly: CPI and PCE. Both track how fast prices are rising across the United States, and both feed directly into expectations for interest rates. They are not the same measurement, though. They come from different agencies, use different baskets of goods and services, and often print slightly different figures for the same month. Knowing where they agree and where they diverge is one of the most practical skills a macro trader can build.

The short version: CPI is the inflation number the public and the media quote, while Core PCE is the inflation number the central bank quotes. A trader who watches only one is reading half the story. When a release lands, the reaction you see on the screen depends on which gauge printed, how far it sat from the consensus forecast, and what the other gauge did in the weeks around it.

The headline

CPI: the number that grabs the headlines

The Consumer Price Index is published by the Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS). It measures the average change in prices paid by urban consumers for a fixed basket of goods and services, from groceries and rent to fuel and medical care. Because it lands around the middle of each month and covers the prior month, it is usually the first comprehensive inflation reading markets get, which is a big reason it dominates the news cycle.

Traders watch two versions. Headline CPI includes everything. Core CPI strips out food and energy, the two categories that swing the most from month to month, to show the underlying trend. A hot core print often matters more for rate expectations than a headline number that was pushed around by a single move in oil.

  • Publisher: Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS).
  • Cadence: monthly, around the middle of the month, covering the prior month.
  • Why it moves markets: first comprehensive read, highly visible, widely quoted by the media.
The Fed's gauge

PCE and Core PCE: what the central bank actually targets

The Personal Consumption Expenditures price index is published by the Bureau of Economic Analysis (BEA), released as part of the monthly Personal Income and Outlays report. It usually arrives later in the month than CPI, which changes how traders treat it.

The detail that matters most: Core PCE, which excludes food and energy, is the Federal Reserve's preferred inflation gauge and the basis for its 2 percent target. When policymakers talk about getting inflation back to target, the number in their mind is Core PCE, not CPI. That single fact is why a PCE surprise can reshape the expected rate path even when it arrives after CPI has already moved the market.

When the Fed Chair describes progress on inflation at a press conference, the reference point is almost always Core PCE. Markets know this, so the gauge carries weight out of proportion to the attention it receives in the mainstream press.

Under the hood

Scope and weighting: why the two gauges rarely match

CPI and PCE often print a few tenths apart, and that is not noise. Three structural differences explain most of the gap.

The table below is a quick reference for the distinctions that matter when you are reading a release in real time.

  • Broader basket. PCE covers a wider range of spending, including purchases made on behalf of households (for example, healthcare paid through employer plans and government programmes), which CPI largely leaves out.
  • Substitution. PCE updates its weights more dynamically to capture how consumers change behaviour when prices shift, such as buying more chicken when beef gets expensive. CPI leans on a more fixed basket, so it does not adjust as quickly.
  • Different weights. The two gauges weight categories differently. Healthcare, for instance, carries far more weight in PCE. These weighting choices are a key reason PCE often reads a little lower than CPI over time.

Market impact

Which one moves markets more, and when

In the moment, CPI usually triggers the larger immediate reaction. It arrives first, it is the figure every desk and news outlet is watching, and the gap between the actual print and the consensus forecast tends to produce the sharpest, fastest moves in forex, indices, bonds and gold. The headline hits the wire and prices reprice within seconds.

PCE plays a different role. By the time it lands, the market has often already digested CPI for the same period, so PCE tends to confirm or challenge the trend rather than set it from scratch. But because it is the gauge the Fed actually targets, a clear divergence (PCE cooling while CPI ran hot, or the reverse) can force a rethink of the rate path. The reaction may be smaller on impact yet more durable for the policy outlook.

This is also why both belong on any serious macro trader's radar. On the economic calendar, each release carries an impact tag, a countdown, the previous value and the consensus forecast, so you can see which gauge is due and how much attention it is likely to command before it prints.

The playbook

How to use CPI and PCE together

The two gauges are most useful as a pair. CPI gives you the fast, tradable event and the first read on direction. PCE gives you the confirmation that matters for the central bank, and therefore for the medium-term rate path. Reading them in sequence, rather than treating either in isolation, is how you separate a one-month wobble from a genuine change in the inflation trend.

A practical routine: prepare for the CPI event with a plan for both a beat and a miss, then watch how PCE lines up a couple of weeks later. If both point the same way, the signal for rates is strong. If they conflict, expect the Fed to lean on PCE, and position for the next policy meeting accordingly.

FAQ

Common questions

What is the main difference between PCE and CPI?

Both measure US inflation, but CPI (from the Bureau of Labor Statistics) tracks a fixed basket of prices paid by urban consumers and is the headline number the public quotes, while PCE (from the Bureau of Economic Analysis) covers a broader range of spending and updates its weights more dynamically. Core PCE, which excludes food and energy, is the Federal Reserve's preferred gauge and the basis for its 2 percent target.

Why is PCE usually lower than CPI?

PCE tends to read a little lower mainly because of scope and weighting. It captures substitution (consumers shifting to cheaper alternatives as prices change) and it weights categories such as healthcare differently from CPI. Those structural choices typically leave PCE a few tenths below CPI over time, though the gap varies month to month.

Which inflation number does the Federal Reserve use?

The Federal Reserve targets Core PCE, the Personal Consumption Expenditures price index excluding food and energy. Its 2 percent inflation goal is defined in PCE terms. When the Fed Chair discusses progress on inflation, the reference point is almost always Core PCE rather than CPI.

Does CPI or PCE move markets more?

CPI usually triggers the larger immediate reaction because it is released first, around the middle of the month, and is the most visible inflation figure. PCE arrives later and tends to confirm or challenge the trend, but it can still reshape rate expectations because it is the gauge the Fed actually targets.

When are CPI and PCE released each month?

CPI is published by the BLS around the middle of the month, covering the prior month. PCE is published by the BEA later in the month as part of the Personal Income and Outlays report.
