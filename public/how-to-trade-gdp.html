How to Trade

Gross Domestic Product is the broadest read on the economy. Here is how the quarterly release works, why the first estimate usually matters most, and how to prepare for the move.

The release

What GDP measures and why traders watch it

Gross Domestic Product (GDP) is the single broadest measure of economic output. It adds up the value of everything an economy produces over a set period, so a strong number signals expansion and a weak one points to a slowdown or contraction. Because almost every other data point ultimately feeds into this one figure, traders treat GDP as the headline scorecard for how an economy is actually performing.

In the United States the Bureau of Economic Analysis (BEA) publishes GDP quarterly, and it does so in three separate estimates for each quarter. The data crosses at 8:30 AM ET, the same slot used for many of the most market-moving American releases, so each quarter is revisited three times before the picture is considered settled.

GDP is reported as an annualized growth rate, so a print described as 2.5 percent means the economy would grow by that amount over a full year if the quarter's pace held. For a trade, the level itself is rarely the point. What matters is the gap between the figure and what the market expected, because price already reflects the consensus forecast before the release lands.

The three reads

Advance, second and third estimates

The three estimates are not three different quarters. They are three progressively more complete looks at the same quarter. The advance estimate arrives first, roughly a month after the quarter closes, and it is built on incomplete source data. The second estimate and the third estimate follow in the two months after, each folding in fuller information.

For trading purposes the advance estimate usually carries the most weight. It is the first official read, so it contains the largest amount of fresh information and tends to produce the sharpest reaction. By the time the second and third estimates appear, much of the quarter is already understood, so they often pass with only a modest move. The exception is a large revision: if a later estimate shifts the growth rate meaningfully, or revises a key component, it can still move markets because it changes the story the first read told.

  • Advance estimate: first read, least complete data, usually the biggest reaction.
  • Second estimate: more source data folded in, typically a smaller move.
  • Third estimate: the most complete read, often quiet unless the revision is large.
Inside the number

The components that drive the headline

The headline growth rate is the sum of four broad spending categories, plus a price measure that doubles as an inflation signal. Knowing where the strength or weakness came from matters as much as the top-line figure, because the market reads the mix for clues about momentum heading into the next quarter.

Consumer spending is by far the largest piece in most developed economies, so a surprise there tends to drive the headline. Business investment signals corporate confidence, government spending adds a steadier contribution, and net exports (exports minus imports) can swing the number sharply when trade flows shift. Sitting inside the report is the GDP price deflator, a broad gauge of prices across the whole economy that traders watch as a second inflation reading alongside the growth story.

Market reaction

How GDP moves the dollar, indices and yields

GDP feeds trades through two linked channels: growth and policy expectations. A hot number says the economy is running strong, which usually lifts the US dollar and can support equity indices on the growth story, while also pushing bond yields higher as traders price in a central bank that may need to stay restrictive. A cold number tends to work the other way, weighing on the dollar and yields as rate-cut expectations build.

The deflator complicates that clean picture. A soft growth figure paired with a hot deflator points to weaker output with stickier prices, a mix that can leave the dollar and yields pulled in different directions as the market weighs growth against inflation. Reading the beat or miss on both the headline and the deflator, rather than the headline alone, is what separates a considered reaction from a reflexive one.

Backward-looking

Why GDP often reacts more quietly than NFP or CPI

GDP carries an important quirk: it is backward-looking. By the time the advance estimate lands, the quarter it describes has already ended, and the market has spent three months reading the monthly data that feeds into it. Much of the result is therefore anticipated, which is why the reaction to GDP can be more muted than the reaction to fresher monthly figures.

That contrast is clearest against the fast monthly releases. The jobs report is a snapshot of the most recent month and lands with little warning on the labor side, so it tends to move markets hard, as covered in how to trade NFP. Inflation data is similarly timely and policy-sensitive, which is why how to trade CPI is one of the most watched events on the calendar. GDP sits a step behind both in freshness, so a trader should size expectations accordingly.

None of that makes GDP safe to ignore. A large surprise in the headline, or a hot deflator that reframes the inflation outlook, can still trigger a sharp, fast move, and the advance estimate is the most likely of the three to deliver one. The lesson is to respect the release without assuming it will always be quiet.

Preparation

Preparing for the release and managing risk

Preparation for GDP looks much like preparation for any high-impact event, with extra emphasis on knowing which of the three estimates is due. Before the print, the key reference points are the consensus forecast, the previous reading, and any recent monthly data that hints at how the quarter shaped up. Checking the economic calendar in advance tells you the exact date, the estimate type, and the market's expectation, and planning the full quarter around the 2026-2027 release dates removes the risk of being caught off guard.

On the risk side, GDP days can bring a thin, jumpy tape in the seconds around 8:30 AM ET, with spreads widening as liquidity thins. A few practical habits reduce the damage from a bad fill or a false move.

  • Know in advance whether the advance, second or third estimate is due, and weight your expectations accordingly.
  • Note both the headline consensus and the deflator, not just the growth number.
  • Size positions for the wider spreads and faster moves that can follow the print.
  • Decide your plan before the release rather than reacting into the first spike.

FAQ

Common questions

What time is US GDP released?

In the United States the Bureau of Economic Analysis publishes GDP at 8:30 AM ET, the same slot used for many major American data releases. It comes out quarterly, and each quarter is released three times as the advance, second and third estimates.

Which GDP estimate matters most for trading?

The advance estimate usually moves markets most because it is the first official read on the quarter and contains the most fresh information. The second and third estimates tend to pass more quietly, unless a later revision changes the growth rate or a key component by a large amount.

Is GDP more important than NFP or CPI for traders?

Not usually in terms of the immediate reaction. GDP is backward-looking, so much of it is anticipated by the time it lands, and the move can be more muted than the monthly jobs or inflation reports. A large surprise or a hot deflator can still produce a sharp move, so it should be respected rather than ignored.

What is the GDP price deflator?

The GDP price deflator is a broad measure of price change across the whole economy, reported inside the GDP release. Traders read it as a second inflation signal alongside the growth figure, and a hot deflator can move the dollar and bond yields even when the headline growth rate is soft.
